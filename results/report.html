TCP vs UDP Performance Analyzer

8 network scenarios · paced load 800 × 1024 B at 400 msg/s (3.28 Mbps) · bulk 1500 messages · backend: userspace link emulator (TCP loss modelled as retransmission delay) · 2026-11-10 13:06:55

Key findings

  • On a clean link both deliver 100%; UDP's median one-way delay is 0.31 ms vs 0.38 ms for TCP. With no impairment the gap is pure processing overhead (stream framing, ACKs, the relay), not the network.
  • high-latency: both see ~80 ms delay, but TCP bulk throughput collapsed from 1262 to 3.2 Mbps: a 64 KB window over a 160 ms RTT allows at most 3.3 Mbps. UDP, which waits for no ACKs, still pushed 134 Mbps.
  • jitter: with ±15 ms jitter, 582 of 800 UDP datagrams arrived out of order. TCP had none, but waiting for late segments lifted its median delay to 45 ms vs 30 ms for UDP.
  • loss-1: at 1% loss UDP lost 1.2% of messages and kept p95 at 21 ms. TCP delivered 100%, but retransmissions and head-of-line blocking pushed its p95 to 48 ms and cut bulk throughput to 10.1 Mbps.
  • loss-5: at 5% loss UDP lost 5.9% of messages and kept p95 at 22 ms. TCP delivered 100%, but retransmissions and head-of-line blocking pushed its p95 to 256 ms and cut bulk throughput to 5.3 Mbps.
  • loss-15: at 15% loss UDP lost 16.2% of messages and kept p95 at 22 ms. TCP delivered 100%, but retransmissions and head-of-line blocking pushed its p95 to 1227 ms and cut bulk throughput to 1.6 Mbps.
  • bottleneck: offered 3.28 Mbps into a 2 Mbps link. UDP has no congestion control, so the router dropped 37% of it. TCP lost nothing but slowed the sender: its median delay grew to 704 ms as data queued.
  • bottleneck (bulk): TCP filled the bottleneck at 1.95 Mbps with 100% delivery; blasting UDP got 1.70 Mbps through but only 2.2% of what it sent arrived.
  • mobile: under combined impairments UDP delivered 95.9% (9 corrupted, 6 duplicated, 603 reordered) with p95 73 ms; TCP delivered 100%, intact and in order, with p95 436 ms.
  • Takeaway: choose TCP when every byte must arrive intact and in order (files, web, databases). Choose UDP when late data is useless (voice, video, games, DNS) and the application can tolerate or repair loss itself.

Method

The client sends sequence-numbered, CRC-protected, timestamped messages to the server through a link emulator. The server records every arrival and the client fetches those records over a separate control channel that bypasses the emulator. Delay is one-way (sender and receiver share a clock), jitter is the mean change in delay between consecutive arrivals, goodput counts only unique, intact payload bytes, and "in-order" counts messages delivered exactly once, intact and not overtaken.

The emulator applies the same profile to both protocols. UDP datagrams are dropped, corrupted, duplicated, delayed independently (so they reorder) and tail-dropped when the router queue is full. A userspace proxy cannot drop bytes from a TCP stream, so each lost or corrupted TCP segment is instead delayed by what TCP's recovery costs: +1 RTT for fast retransmit, then exponential RTO backoff from 200 ms. Segments are released in order (head-of-line blocking), and a 64 KB window freed by returning ACKs limits throughput to window/RTT. Congestion-window reduction is not modelled. Run --backend netem as root on Linux to measure the kernel's real TCP instead.
